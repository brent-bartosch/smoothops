## Verdict
Do not lead with the mechanism teardown. Lead every post and page with a buyer-language diagnosis or result, and put a plain-English mechanism underneath as the proof, drawn only from systems Smoothed built and governs (Option C, in the "tear down the buyer's problem, not the platform's plumbing" form). The Balfe topic itself, automating a platform against its terms, is out entirely.
Confidence: Medium because both briefs independently concede the same ground and the dealbreaker evidence is primary-verified, but nobody has measured teardown-led against outcome-led content for these buyers, so the ordering rests on inference from general buyer surveys.

## The cruxes it turned on
1. Is the "peer" reader of a teardown inside the buying group, carrying Smoothed's name upstairs (FOR), or a builder who never hires (AGAINST)? → Favors AGAINST, moderately. FOR's case leans on Edelman–LinkedIn 2025 "hidden buyers", but the report defines them as final decision-makers from finance, operations, legal, compliance and procurement who do not need in-depth knowledge of the product. They are the opposite of a RevOps builder, and the least likely people to follow an HTTP-level walkthrough. FOR's other support, the Explorium "walk me through the system and name the endpoints" test, is advice for a hiring manager interviewing an employee candidate, not for a CRO reading a consultant's blog. Neither brief found data on how often a CMO or CRO delegates vendor vetting to RevOps, and I found none. What survives for FOR: Head of RevOps is on the frame's own list of signers and is plausibly a teardown reader. Key source: Edelman definition as quoted by CustomerThink (primary PDF blocked).
2. What moves the economic buyer: an explainer that makes them more AI-literate (FOR), or outcome, proof and a named problem (AGAINST)? → Favors AGAINST, moderately. Wynter's 100-CMO survey (read directly) says shortlists run on brand recognition (79%) and peers in private communities (72%), and that an unknown vendor gets in through peer recommendation, a low-friction pilot (38%) and "real ROI in a short pilot". It reports nothing on content format. SurveyMonkey/Reddit (read directly) puts peer recommendations at 73% trust against 36% for social media, with "finding real user testimonials" the top obstacle (48%). Edelman's 91% figure is for content that uncovers a challenge the buyer had not recognised. On FOR's AI-literacy point, the Gartner advice as reported by Marketing Dive is about use cases tied to measurable outcomes, model limits, validating outputs and holding agencies accountable for governance. That supports "explained simply" at the level of use, limits and governance. It does not support protocol-level plumbing. No survey on either side tested mechanism explainers specifically.
3. Is a teardown the one proof that cannot be faked without client names (FOR), or the house style of the crowded field (AGAINST)? → Split, and it decides the layer, not the lead. FOR is right that buyers want testimonials and peer proof Smoothed cannot supply by name, so demonstrated understanding is the best substitute available. That is an argument for mechanism as evidence under a claim. AGAINST's "house style" point is opinion with one weakly sourced figure. AGAINST's star witness also cuts both ways: Jason Liu says clients care about outcomes over implementation, yet in the same post names a technical piece ("RAG is more than embeddings") as one of the biggest catalysts for his consulting business. His buyers were technical leaders, so it does not transfer cleanly to CMOs and CROs, but AGAINST cited him selectively.
4. Does the reference topic trip dealbreaker 2? → Not a real crux; both briefs agree, and it is verified. LinkedIn's help page (read directly) prohibits third-party software that scrapes or automates activity. The Balfe post (read directly) never mentions the terms and lists the evasion layers (proxies, fingerprints, warming, impossible travel). One correction to AGAINST: the post opens "If you sell for a living", so it is aimed at salespeople, not builders. FOR's reading of the audience is the accurate one. The topic is still out.

Evidence audit, in brief:
- Shared sources read differently. Both briefs cite the 6sense 2025 report. FOR reads "ranked before contact" as "whoever read beforehand decides"; AGAINST reads it as "you must be the pre-ranked favourite on proof". The report says nothing about which content produces the ranking, so it supports neither reading over the other. Both cite Edelman 2025 and ColdIQ; both read the Balfe post.
- Figures from secondary coverage, not the primary: every Edelman 2024 and 2025 number (primary returned 403 for both briefs and for me); the 6sense 61/94/80/77 figures (press release 403; confirmed in CustomerThink's summary; 6sense's own page confirms only the sample of just under 4,000 plus 766); TrustRadius 94%; the Gartner "15% AI-savvy" figure; Forrester (headline only); Dataiku/Harris.
- Corrections. AGAINST's "buyers initiate contact about 80%" misreads the figure: 79% initiated contact, and 80% is the share who contacted their top-ranked vendor first, which is how FOR read it. Immaterial to the outcome. FOR's Edelman 51% and 52% figures did not appear in the secondary source I checked; could not verify. The Gartner 15% is a CEO-perception figure whose source survey Marketing Dive does not name; could not verify against Gartner.
- Sample caveat neither brief raised: 48% of Edelman 2025 respondents were at companies of 200 or fewer employees. That fits Smoothed's Series A–B targets reasonably well, less so the PE-backed ones.
- Stale: Edelman 2024 (fielded Feb 2024), Refine Labs podcast (Aug 2021), Jason Liu (Apr 2025, and marked archival: he no longer takes consulting work), MarTech on Apollo/Seamless (May 2025).
- Opinion-heavy and discounted: FOR's criterion 3 and 5 (vendor blogs, self-reported agency stories with inconsistent numbers); AGAINST's argument 2 (marketing blogs, one firsthand account) and the PE-screening inference in argument 3, which AGAINST flags as its own.

## Strongest case against the recommendation
Smoothed cannot name clients, and the surveys say buyers trust peers and testimonials far more than vendor claims. "CAC down 90%" with no name attached is exactly what every relabeled outbound agency posts, so an outcome-led top line risks being the least believable sentence on the page. A teardown is the only asset that proves the work without a logo, and a former VP of Sales explaining a pipeline mechanism in sales language is rarer than either an engineer doing it or a sales leader posting results. If the buyer-language headline reads like everyone else's, the reader never reaches the mechanism underneath, and Option C collapses into the generic positioning it was meant to avoid. This risk is real and is why the mechanism layer has to be visible in the first screen, not buried.

## What would flip this
- Smoothed's own numbers: over 6–8 weeks, alternate teardown-led and diagnosis-led posts and log inbound by title. If teardown-led posts produce more conversations with director-and-above marketing, sales or RevOps people, lead with teardowns.
- A named solo practitioner or small firm selling multi-month engagements to CMOs, CROs or RevOps heads who attributes signed work to mechanism-level explainers, with who signed stated. Neither brief found one in about 30 combined searches, and my targeted search found none.
- A decision to treat the Head of RevOps or first GTM engineer as the primary signer rather than one of five. That reader is semi-technical and the balance shifts toward teardown-led.
- In the other direction: if posts with the mechanism layer draw no more buyer engagement than posts without it, drop the layer from LinkedIn and keep it for long-form and sales follow-up only.

## Option C
Both briefs converge on it: AGAINST proposes it outright, and FOR concedes its inference "would be equally well served by alternative C". It beats both pure positions.

- Top line of a post or page: a diagnosis or result in the buyer's words. "Where $81K a year of ad spend was leaking, and how we found it." "Why a campaign launch took 8 hours, and what took it to 3 minutes." "What a qualified lead costs when the signal does the qualifying: under five cents."
- Underneath: the mechanism in plain English, three to six steps, each term glossed in a clause the way Balfe does it, followed by what broke, the limits, and the guardrail. End with the governance point (who approves, what is logged, what data is touched), because the Wynter CMOs called security and compliance non-negotiable for an unknown vendor.
- The sales background goes in the framing: "as someone who carried quota for seven years, here is the number I would have wanted".
- In: the signals pipeline, the outbound launch engine, the ad-spend audit, the customer-risk model, cost per qualified lead, failure modes, data handling, how to tell a working system from a demo.
- Out: LinkedIn automation internals, session cookies, proxies, scraping or detection-avoidance of any kind; teardowns of third-party tools' infrastructure (the "how Exa works" type) as a lead; builder-opinion pieces (MCP and similar) on the buyer channel.
- One caution from me, not from either brief: the "240,000 retailers at 72% email discovery" proof invites the question of how the data was sourced. Any teardown of it should state the sources and their terms up front, or leave that one as a headline number only.
- Cadence for one person: one long-form teardown of an own system a month, cut into the week's posts, each opening on the buyer's problem. Option D (separate builder channel) is not worth the doubled load.
